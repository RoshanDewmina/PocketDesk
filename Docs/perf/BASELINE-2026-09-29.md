Numbers come from two logs the orchestrator collected with Roshan (`~/Downloads/PocketDeskStreamStats.jsonl`, the phone's export, 136 one-second samples with the Mac's per-second summary embedded; `~/Library/Caches/PocketDeskStreamStats.jsonl`, the Mac's own log, of which the last three 2560-wide sessions are this morning's) and from the orchestrator's `bench/analyze.py` run on the 80 s phone screen recording. Evidence labels: **[M]** measured in these logs, **[S]** read in source, **[E]** inference that needs a test.

## Finding 1: the encode number is latency, not work, and it is bimodal

What the logs prove:

- `encodeMs` does not track bitrate, QP, key frames or capture health: correlation with `sentKbps` −0.01, with `qpAverage` −0.17, with `targetKbps` +0.15; the per-second values hop between 30 and 42 ms with no change in any of those (Mac sessions 1–2, 90 cluster changes, none coinciding with a key frame). Size was 2560×1656 in every sample.
- 58 fps was sustained *while* `encodeMs` read 30–42 ms (phone samples 0–35 and 95–113). A hardware encoder that needed 40 ms of work per frame could not encode 58 of them a second; the figure is therefore time a frame spends **inside VideoToolbox before its callback** (WebRTC stamps encode start immediately before `Encode()` and encode finish in the encoded-image callback [S]), i.e. queueing or pipeline depth, not the per-frame cost.
- The low state is real and recent: **every fresh VideoToolbox session started at ≈14.7 ms** (Mac session 1 rows 1–7 after connect; rows 19–21 right after the `DesktopH264Encoder` restart, which recreates the session) and then rose to 30–42 ms within 3–8 s. The session after Roshan's reconnect (Mac session 3) **stayed at 14.7 ms for 165 s at 58 fps with `qualityLimitation = none`** on the same content and settings.
- The 38 fps periods are WebRTC's encode-usage overuse detector cutting the frame rate because encode time ÷ frame interval exceeded its threshold (`maintainResolution` keeps pixels and drops frames [S]). At 38 fps the measured latency fell to 23–26 ms, i.e. roughly one frame period, which is what a full pipeline looks like when it is fed exactly at its drain rate. Note that even the good state, 14.7 / 17.2 ms = 85 %, sits on the detector's threshold.

What the logs cannot decide, and how to decide it:

| Hypothesis | Fits | Test |
|---|---|---|
| H-A **Contention on the media engine or GPU from another process** (the landing pass's simulator/UI test runs, iPhone Mirroring, Safari's full-screen 60 Hz stimulus page, a second Farside host from an E2E run) during 07:0x–07:16 but not during session 3 | The bad state is environmental and ended with the morning's other work; encode was slow from a few seconds after connect | Repeat on a quiet Mac (no builds, simulators, browsers, iPhone Mirroring) with the per-frame encoder trace (below) and `top -l 1 -o cpu` sampled beside it |
| H-B **A VideoToolbox session state** (rate control at a 20 Mb/s target with 0.2 Mb/s of content, or an effort mode) that raises pipeline depth after a few seconds and resets with the session | Each new session was fast for a few seconds; the restart at row 19 gave 3 fast seconds | A/B the encoder ceiling (25 vs 12 Mb/s on Sharper) and Responsive (1920) on the phone; the trace shows whether in-flight frames grow |
| H-C **Real content is simply heavier than the loopback's synthetic text** (2.3 ms/MP was measured on synthetic frames) | Explains 14.7 vs the 10–12 ms estimate, not the 30–42 ms state | Trace bytes per frame beside latency |

The per-frame encoder trace is the instrument to add: `DesktopH264Encoder` already wraps `encode()` and the encoded-image callback, so it can record submit time, callback time, frames in flight at submit, bytes and frame type per frame, and report `encodeLatencyP50/P90`, `encodeInFlightMax` and `encodeBytesP50` per second in the host summary. Frames in flight > 1 at 58 fps proves queueing; in flight = 1 with 40 ms proves per-frame latency inside the engine.

Not yet a root cause. Do not change the encoder path on this evidence; the same path ran at 14.7 ms an hour into the morning.

## Finding 2: "host sent 57, phone received 40" is a sampling artefact

The Mac's `sentFPS` equals its `encodedFPS` in all 190 samples (difference 0.0 min to max). The phone's `receivedFPS` matches the Mac's `encodedFPS` embedded in the same phone sample with p50 0.0 and p90 +3 fps; the only differences over 10 fps are the two seconds where the rate changed (sample 94: phone 51 vs Mac 38, the recovery second) and the disconnect (samples 115–127: phone 0 while the last Mac summary still says 59). The Mac summary rides the once-per-second capture heartbeat and is up to one sample older than the phone's window, so the overlay can pair a 57 fps Mac second with a 40 fps phone second at a transition. Frames were not lost; the overlay line should show the summary's age.

## Finding 3: a ≥ 98 ms arrival gap in every second at the phone

While decoded ≥ 30 fps: the phone's largest inter-arrival gap per one-second window is p10 98 ms, p50 108 ms, p90 144 ms, max 264 ms (115 of 115 windows have a gap ≥ 80 ms). On the same seconds the Mac's capture gap p90 is 17.9 ms, its pacer delay p50 0 ms, and loss is 0 %. Received frames therefore leave the Mac on time and arrive at the phone's decoder late in bunches: the same seconds show 9–20 superseded frames/s (two decoded frames between two 120 Hz draws) and a jitter-buffer *target* of 150–260 ms (the estimator sees the jitter even though playout is forced to 0). RTT p90 is 49–63 ms with spikes to 97–165 ms.

A once-a-second, ~100 ms interruption with zero loss is the documented AWDL signature (PLAYBOOK H5): the radio leaves the Wi-Fi channel for AWDL availability windows on a roughly one-second cycle, and iPhone Mirroring, AirDrop and Handoff keep AWDL active on both devices. Whether it is AWDL, the access point, or something once-a-second in the apps (the statistics timers, the stats log write) is decided by the marker instrument (distinct-marker gap histogram) plus an A/B: Mac on Ethernet or `awdl0` down, phone with AirDrop receiving off and iPhone Mirroring closed. This gap, not decode or draw, is what caps p95 today.

## What is fine

Decode 7 ms and decoded→draw 6 ms on the phone; jitter buffer 0.9 ms; capture 58 fps with 1 ms display→callback latency; level 5.2 negotiated and 2560×1656 delivered; 120 Hz requested and reported (its real cadence is measured by the presented-handler instrument, not yet by this log).
